# B1 — Choose and Create: Design Document

## Category Purpose

Choose and Create at B1 asks students to design, plan, or improve something realistic for a clear end user or purpose. Students are not inventing freely — they are solving a practical design problem within limits, and justifying why their solution is the best one available.

This category moves students beyond simple selection (A1/A2 "choose and create" = picking items) into evaluation and planning: weighing options, prioritising what matters most, and defending a finished plan.

Students may create something new, improve something that already exists, or organise information into a better solution.

The emphasis is always on producing something useful for a real purpose.
---

## Design Rule

Every activity must have:

- a real end user (a team, a family, a visitor, a friend, a colleague — someone the plan is *for*)
- a realistic constraint (budget, time, space, number of people, existing conditions)
- a decision to justify, not just a description to give

Students should think:

"What is the best solution given these limits?"

not

"What could I imagine or invent?"

If a prompt could be answered without picking between real trade-offs, it is not ready.

---

## Good Examples

- You are planning the layout of a small office for a team of five. Decide where things go and explain your reasoning.
- A community centre has a fixed budget for a weekend event. Decide how to spend it and justify your choices.
- Create a weekly schedule for someone with only a few free hours. Prioritise what matters most.
- Choose the equipment needed for a small project, working within a limited budget.
- An existing plan has a problem. Improve it and explain what you changed and why.

---

## Avoid

- fantasy or impossible creations ("design your dream house," "if you had unlimited money...")
- open-ended imagination with no constraint or user
- creating purely for its own sake, with no purpose behind the design
- interview-style questions disguised as "choose" tasks ("What would you put in your ideal room?")
- tasks with only one obviously correct answer

---

## Language Focus

Students should naturally reach for:

- I would recommend...
- The best option is...
- I decided to... because...
- Instead of X, I chose Y because...
- Given the budget/time/space, ...
- This works better because...
- One problem with the original plan was... so I changed it to...
- Although X is possible, Y is more practical because...

---

## Prompt Structure

Each prompt should follow this shape:

1. **Situation** — a realistic context with a named end user
2. **Constraint** — a limit (budget, time, space, number of people, existing plan)
3. **Task** — design, plan, choose, or improve something specific
4. **Justification hook** — explicitly ask the student to explain or defend their choice

Example skeleton:

"[End user] needs [thing to plan/create], but [constraint]. Decide [specific choices] and explain why."

---

## Quality Check

Before a prompt is accepted, confirm:

- [ ] Is there a clear end user or purpose (not creation for its own sake)?
- [ ] Is there a realistic constraint that forces trade-offs?
- [ ] Does the task require a decision, not just a description?
- [ ] Does the prompt explicitly ask for justification ("explain why," "why is this the best option")?
- [ ] Is the scenario grounded and realistic — no fantasy, no impossible resources?
- [ ] Could a student answer this by only listing things, with no reasoning? If yes, revise.
- [ ] Does it feel like a planning task, not an interview question?
