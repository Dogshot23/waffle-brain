# B1 Everyday Situations — Design Document

## Category Purpose

At A1/A2, Everyday Situations helped students talk about familiar real-life topics: routines, home, food, travel, hobbies, daily activities. The goal was simple description and comfortable, structured speech.

At B1, the category keeps the same real-world territory — home, money, work, daily life, local services, family logistics — but the student's job changes. They are no longer just describing their life. They are managing it.

Every B1 Everyday Situations prompt should place the student inside a realistic everyday problem that has constraints and requires a decision. The student should leave the activity having compared options, made a choice, and explained why.

## Design Rule

B1 does not increase imagination. It increases judgement.

Students should rarely be asked to invent a scenario or picture something unreal. Instead they respond to a grounded, plausible situation drawn from ordinary adult life, and think it through.

Every prompt should give the student:

- a clear, realistic goal
- limited choices or constraints (money, time, options, people involved)
- a reason to justify whatever they decide

The internal test for every prompt: does it make the student think "What would I actually do here?" — not "What imaginary story can I invent?"

## Good Examples

"You have enough money to fix ONE thing in your apartment this month. Choose what to fix and explain why."

"Your internet company offers two plans: one cheaper but slower, one faster but more expensive. Which do you choose for your household, and why?"

"You need to reschedule a dentist appointment, but the only free slots clash with work. Decide what to do and explain your reasoning."

"Your family is deciding between eating at home or ordering food tonight. Make the decision and justify it."

Each example: a real situation, a limited set of options, and a decision that needs a reason.

## Avoid

- Interview-style questions with a single factual answer ("What do you usually eat for breakfast?")
- Pure description with no decision to make ("Describe your daily routine.")
- Fictional or fantastical premises ("Imagine you live on a spaceship...")
- Abstract or philosophical framing ("What does home mean to you?")
- Debate or opinion-only prompts disconnected from a concrete situation
- Scenarios with only one sensible answer, which shut down discussion
- Tasks that can be completed with a single word or short phrase

## Language Focus

Prompts in this category should naturally pull students toward:

- because / so / since
- although / but / on the other hand
- if / unless
- should / could / might
- I would recommend...
- I think the best option is...
- It depends on...
- Compared to..., ...
- The main problem is...

## Prompt Structure

Each prompt should follow this shape:

1. **Situation** — a short, realistic everyday context (1–2 sentences)
2. **Constraint** — a limit that forces a real trade-off (money, time, choice between two things, competing priorities)
3. **Task** — a clear instruction to decide, choose, or solve
4. **Justification hook** — an explicit or implied "explain why," so the student must reason, not just answer

The `constraint` field (as used elsewhere in the app's schema) should capture the boundary condition that keeps the task realistic and speakable within a short activity — e.g. "Choose only one," "You must pick a specific time," "Give at least one reason."

## Quality Check

Before a prompt is accepted into this category, it should pass all of the following:

- [ ] Is the situation something a real adult could plausibly face?
- [ ] Does the student have to choose between at least two options?
- [ ] Is there a built-in reason to justify the choice?
- [ ] Could this be answered in one word? (If yes, rewrite.)
- [ ] Does it avoid fictional or fantastical framing?
- [ ] Does it avoid open philosophical or abstract questions?
- [ ] Does it feel like a task, not an interview question?
- [ ] Would a teacher be able to run this live with minimal setup?
